\begin{abstract}
Drift scanning, in which a dish is parked at fixed azimuth and elevation while the sky rotates through the beam, keeps ground and spillover pickup stationary, making it an attractive strategy for single-dish 21\,cm intensity mapping with SKA-Mid. We compare it with a MeerKLASS-style constant-elevation raster in controlled simulations of a 15\,m dish at the Karoo site, reconstructing maps with a Wiener filter and isolating the residual that survives noiseless data. This beam\,+\,prior floor, the sky a strategy never measures, is 96--99 per cent of the total residual in every configuration tested and is unchanged by integration time, system temperature or the number of co-pointed dishes. A parked dish cannot cross-link at any azimuth: every track runs at position angle $90^\circ$, leaving the declination ladder as its only geometric freedom. Counting constrained modes without a threshold as $N_\mathrm{eff}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbfss{WA})$, we find mode count necessary but not sufficient. At 350--400\,MHz a twelve-strip drift matches the raster's mode density to 2.2 per cent, yet its floor is 9.3 times higher; the gap persists at matched depth and is not explained by angular scale. Because all drift tracks share one orientation, the unmeasured modes correlate across frequency: the floor is spectrally non-smooth, needs 2--6 principal components where the foregrounds need one, and overlaps the H\,\textsc{i} subspace, so no cleaning depth improves the net recovery. The result holds in all three bands tested up to 725\,MHz, although the drift--raster ordering inverts near 600\,MHz. In survey terms, detecting the cross-power spectrum between the \textsc{Hi} map and an ideal external tracer ($r = 1$, negligible shot noise) at $1\sigma$ needs $\sim$\num{2950}\,deg$^2$ for the raster and $\sim$\num{26500}\,deg$^2$ at $3\sigma$, the latter close to the \num{30717}\,deg$^2$ the site can reach; a parked drift needs more than the whole sky, and still $\sim8$ times the reachable sky once twelve strips bring it to the raster's mode density. In the auto-spectrum the floor is a bias rather than a variance, so no sky area suffices at all.
\end{abstract}

\section{Simulations}
\label{sec:sims}

In this section we describe the simulated instrument, sky and time-ordered
data (TOD), and the two scan strategies that are compared throughout the rest
of the paper.

\subsection{Instrument and sky}
We simulate a single \SI{15}{\metre} dish located at the Karoo site
($\phi = \ang{-30.713}$), sampling every \SI{2}{\second} for a total
integration time of \SI{3}{\hour}. Maps are pixelised using HEALPix at
$N_{\mathrm{side}} = 64$. For the sky model we use GSM2008, rotated to
equatorial coordinates.
% TODO cite: HEALPix (Gorski et al. 2005); GSM2008 (de Oliveira-Costa et al. 2008)
We consider two beam models: a symmetric Gaussian with
$\mathrm{FWHM} = 1.22\lambda/D = \ang{3.99}$ at \SI{350}{\mega\hertz}, and a
tapered circular aperture with a $-\SI{14}{\deci\bel}$ edge taper, which has
$\mathrm{FWHM} = \ang{3.84}$ and a first sidelobe at $\sim-\SI{23}{\deci\bel}$.

\subsection{Time-ordered data}
The TOD are generated without a receiver temperature or any additive
$T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ term, so that the only signal present is the sky. The data
model is then
\begin{equation}
  d(t) = \bigl[ A\,s \bigr](t)\,\bigl(1 + g(t)\bigr)\bigl(1 + \eta(t)\bigr),
  \label{eq:tod}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the pointing-and-beam operator, $g$ is a $1/f$ gain drift drawn
from a pure $f^{-2}$ flicker model, and $\eta$ is white noise with a fractional
variance of $\num{2.5e-6}$. Both noise terms are \emph{fractional}, so the
contamination they introduce scales with the sky brightness.
% TODO: note the seeding scheme -- identical realisations across scenarios is
% what makes every comparison in this paper controlled.

\subsection{Scan strategies}
\label{sec:strategies}
We compare two scan strategies, both of which produce exactly 5400 samples, so
that all comparisons are made at a fixed observing time. For the
\textbf{drift} scan, the dish is parked at an azimuth of \ang{0}, at elevations
of \ang{52}/\ang{50}/\ang{48} on three consecutive sidereal days. For the
\textbf{raster} scan, we use a MeerKLASS-style constant-elevation scan at an
elevation of \ang{38.19}, with the azimuth following a triangle wave of
$\pm\ang{7.5}$ about \ang{45} (rising) and \ang{315} (setting), at a scan speed
of \SI{6}{\arcminute\per\second}.
% TODO cite: MeerKLASS scan strategy

\subsection{Cross-linking and drift scans}
\label{sec:crosslink}
An important difference between the two strategies is that only the raster
can cross-link, i.e.\ observe the same sky along more than one scan direction.
A dish at fixed $(\mathrm{az}, \mathrm{el})$ is stationary in the rotating
frame of the Earth, so its boresight traces out a circle of constant
declination, swept in the direction of increasing RA. The position angle of
the track is therefore \ang{90} at \emph{every} azimuth, which we have verified
numerically at azimuths of $0/45/90/270/315$ degrees; the choice of azimuth
determines which declination is observed, but not the scan direction. In
contrast, the two raster passes cross at an angle of \ang{73.9}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scantracks}), which the drift cannot achieve at any pointing.

\begin{figure*}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{meerklass_scan_tracks.png}
\caption{The two constant-elevation passes --- rising at az \ang{45}, setting
at az \ang{315} --- with the drift patch of Sec.~\ref{sec:sims} overlaid, such

track in Fig.~\ref{fig:laddergeom} instead runs at position angle \ang{90},

\label{fig:scantracks}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Dish count and the declination ladder}
\label{sec:dishladder}
We next consider whether adding dishes can make up for the lack of
cross-linking. Adding dishes at the \emph{same} pointing adds rows to $d$, but
not to $A$. The extra samples are repeat measurements of sky modes that are
already in the row space of $A$, so they reduce the variance of the modes that
the strategy measures, but leave the unmeasured subspace unchanged. This is
equivalent to integrating for longer, and neither changes the beam + prior
floor of Section~\ref{sec:floor}, which depends on $A$ alone. The one exception
is dishes pointed at \emph{different} elevations, which, following the argument
of Section~\ref{sec:crosslink}, observe different declinations and therefore
do contribute new rows.

Since a dish parked at elevation $\theta$ traces the same declination circle
on every sidereal day, observing $N$ strips on $N$ consecutive sidereal days
and parking $N$ dishes at $N$ elevations for a single pass give the same
operator $A$, as each strip covers the same local sidereal time window in
either case. The three-elevation ladder described above is therefore already
equivalent to a three-dish configuration, and a multi-dish array gains the
ladder in wall-clock time rather than in measured modes. Combined with the
impossibility of cross-linking, this leaves the declination ladder, i.e.\ the
number of strips and their spacing in units of the beam, as the only geometric
freedom available to a drift scan. The translation from ladder spacing to sky
coverage is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:laddergeom}.

\centering

\emph{Bottom}: the summed beam response across declination, with the

\section{Map-making}
\label{sec:mapmaking}

In this section we describe how maps are reconstructed from the simulated TOD,
and define the residual that limits the drift scan throughout the rest of the
paper. We use a Wiener filter as the map-maker.
% TODO cite: Wiener-filter / maximum-likelihood map-making, e.g. Tegmark (1997) -- check
With $N$ the noise covariance, $S$ the prior covariance and $\mu$ the prior
mean, the reconstructed map is
\begin{equation}
  m = \bigl(A^{\top} N^{-1} A + S^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}
      \bigl(A^{\top} N^{-1} d + S^{-1}\mu\bigr),
  \label{eq:wiener}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the pointing-and-beam operator and $d$ is the TOD. Since
Eq.~\eqref{eq:wiener} is affine in $d$, it can be written as a fixed linear map,
\begin{equation}
  W = \bigl(A^{\top}N^{-1}A + S^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}A^{\top}N^{-1},
  \label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
plus a constant term that depends only on the prior. We make use of two
consequences of this throughout. First, an injected sky component $s$ adds
linearly to the map, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  m(d + A s) - m(d) = W A s.
  \label{eq:linearity}
\end{equation}
Second, solving for a component's own TOD with $\mu = 0$ returns $WAs$
directly, so that each component requires one solve rather than two. We have
verified Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearity} numerically to a precision of
$5\times10^{-6}$.

\subsection{The beam + prior floor}
\label{sec:floor}

If the map-maker is given \emph{noiseless} data, the reconstruction is still
not exact. The remaining residual is
\begin{equation}
  r_{\mathrm{floor}} = (I - WA)\,s,
  \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
which corresponds to the parts of the sky that the scan strategy never
measured, and which are instead filled in by the prior. Because this term does
not depend on the noise, it does not integrate down with observing time. As we
show in the following sections, it is the quantity that limits every result
in this paper.

\subsection{Choice of prior}
\label{sec:prior}

The prior enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:wiener} in two places, through the mean $\mu$
and through the covariance $S$. These need to be considered separately, since
they affect the reconstruction in different ways. A poor choice of mean can
supply spatial structure that the data never measured, making the recovered
map appear better than the scan strategy allows, while a poor choice of
covariance can shift the boundary between data-dominated and prior-filled
modes, changing both the apparent mode count and the size of the floor.

For the prior mean we use a flat map, $\mu = \bar{s}\,\mathbf{1}$, set to a
constant at the patch mean. No spatial structure is supplied by the prior in
this case, so every spatial mode in the recovered map must come from the data.
This is an important choice. An obvious alternative would be a beam-smoothed
version of the true sky, but this would supply exactly the structure that the
reconstruction is meant to demonstrate. It would also favour the strategy that
measures the least sky: we find a reliance on such a prior of $1.40\times$ for
the drift, against $1.05\times$ for the raster.

For the prior covariance we use a diagonal, isotropic matrix,
$S^{-1} = \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^{-2} I$, where $\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}$ is set to
the variance of the sky over the patch. This carries no angular or spectral
structure, and reduces to a single scalar; it is also the only quantity in
the solve that retains any knowledge of the true sky. The value of this scalar
nevertheless matters, because it sets the point at which the data stop
dominating over the prior. In the eigenbasis of $A^{\top}N^{-1}A$, with
eigenvalues $\lambda_i$, a mode is data-dominated when
$\lambda_i \gg \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^{-2}$, and is otherwise filled in by the
prior. The choice of $\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}$ therefore moves both the measured
mode count of Section~\ref{sec:modes} and the floor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}
together, and any statement of the form ``the survey measures $n$ modes'' is
implicitly conditioned on it. We test this directly in
Fig.~\ref{fig:priorvar}.

\section{Accessible sky modes by strategy}
\label{sec:modes}

In this section we count how many independent sky modes each scan strategy
constrains, using an eigen-analysis of $A^{\top}N^{-1}A$. Writing its
eigenvalues as $\lambda_i$, and using the prior of
Section~\ref{sec:prior}, $S^{-1} = \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^{-2}I$, the map can be
written as $m = WAs + (\text{prior term})$. The resolution matrix $WA$ then
has trace
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} \;=\; \mathrm{tr}(WA)
  \;=\; \sum_i \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i + \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^{-2}},
  \label{eq:neff}
\end{equation}
which is the number of degrees of freedom constrained by the \emph{data}. Each
mode contributes between 0 and 1, depending on whether it is dominated by the
data or by the prior. Eq.~\eqref{eq:neff} has the advantage of requiring no
threshold, and it is exactly complementary to the floor of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}, since $N_{\mathrm{pix}} - N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ counts the
prior-filled directions from which the floor is built. An alternative is to
count the modes above a fixed fraction of $\lambda_{\max}$. We report this
only as a robustness check below, since the drift-to-raster ratio obtained
this way changes depending on where the threshold is placed, and so we do not
use it as our main measure.

Two qualifications apply when comparing Eq.~\eqref{eq:neff} between
strategies. First, the count must be normalised by the observed solid angle. A
narrower beam gains modes but also shrinks the observed patch, so an
unnormalised count would favour observing less sky. Second, and less
obviously, the number of modes per unit sky is only a fair comparison at
\emph{fixed} $\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}$. Relaxing the prior also raises
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, but it does so by reclassifying weakly constrained
directions as data-dominated, rather than by measuring them. Modes gained in
this way do not carry the same information as those gained from the scan
strategy itself.

We find that the count is independent of the pixel grid, giving identical
values at $N_{\mathrm{side}} = 32$, 64, 128 and 256. The results are given in
Table~\ref{tab:modes}.

\section{\textsc{Hi} signal recovery}
\label{sec:hi}

In this section we inject a simulated \textsc{Hi} signal into the TOD, and test
how well it can be recovered from each strategy's maps after foreground
cleaning.

\subsection{Injected signal and geometry}
We generate a log-normal \textsc{Hi} cube at $z_{\mathrm{eff}} = 2.80$. A
Gaussian density field is first multiplied by the \textsc{Hi} bias and
transformed to a log-normal field, after which linear redshift-space
distortions are applied and the field is converted to a brightness temperature
$\Tb(z)$. This gives $\Tb = \SI{0.518}{\milli\kelvin}$ and
$b_{\mathrm{\textsc{Hi}}} = 1.559$.
% TODO cite: log-normal mock method
The cube is placed on the HEALPix grid using a three-dimensional lightcone
interpolation, in which each (pixel, channel) sample is evaluated at its true
comoving position $r(z)\,\hat{n}$.

At $z \simeq 2.8$, a \SI{15}{\metre} dish resolves \SI{401}{\mega\parsec} in the
transverse direction, so that $\kperp$ reaches only
\SI{0.016}{\per\mega\parsec}, while $\kpar$ spans \numrange{0.012}{0.192}. The
accessible three-dimensional $k$-space is therefore a thin sliver close to the
$\kpar$ axis, and for this reason all power spectra below are given as a
function of $\kpar$.

\subsection{Estimator}
We estimate the power spectrum along the line of sight for each pixel and
average over pixels, using
\begin{equation}
  P_{ab}(k) = \frac{\Delta r^{2}}{L\,\overline{w^{2}}}
    \Bigl\langle \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\tilde a(k)\,\tilde b^{*}(k)\bigr]
    \Bigr\rangle_{\mathrm{pix}},
  \qquad k = 2\pi f_{\mathrm{rfft}} .
  \label{eq:pkpar}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde a$ and $\tilde b$ are the Fourier transforms along the line of
sight of the two tapered fields being correlated, $w_i$ is a Blackman taper
applied over $N_c$ channels of comoving depth $\Delta r$, $L = N_c \Delta r$,
and $\overline{w^2}$ is the mean squared taper. The taper is necessary because
the foregrounds are $\sim10^{4}$ times brighter than the \textsc{Hi}, so
leakage from the band edges would otherwise dominate every high-$\kpar$ bin.

\subsection{Foreground cleaning and the transfer function}
We clean the foregrounds using PCA, removing the leading $n$ eigenmodes of the
channel--channel covariance $C = \operatorname{cov}(x - \bar{x})$ of the map
$x$.
% TODO cite: PCA cleaning, e.g. Alonso et al. (2015)
With $U$ the matrix of these eigenvectors, the cleaned map is
\begin{equation}
  x_{\mathrm{clean}} = x - \bigl[ U U^{\top}(x - \bar{x}) + \bar{x} \bigr].
  \label{eq:pca}
\end{equation}
This is the only step in our pipeline that is \emph{not} linear in the data,
and so the resulting signal loss must be measured by injection rather than
computed directly. Following \citet{Cunnington2023}, we define the transfer
function
\begin{equation}
  T(k) = \frac{P\bigl(\mathcal{C}_n[X + X_m] - \mathcal{C}_n[X],\ X_m\bigr)}
              {P(X_m,\,X_m)} .
  \label{eq:tf}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_n$ denotes the clean with $n$ modes removed and $X_m$ is an
independent mock. Using the cross-power in the numerator avoids the positive
noise bias that the auto-power of the difference would carry. Since $X_m$ is
each strategy's own map-made response, $WAs_m$, $T$ is a ratio within a single
strategy, and is therefore independent of depth. The resulting transfer
functions are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tf}.

\subsection{Common resolution}
The beam narrows by \SI{12.5}{\percent} across
\SIrange{350}{400}{\mega\hertz}. We therefore reconvolve each channel to the
widest beam in the band, using a Gaussian kernel of width
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{k}(\nu) = \sqrt{\theta_{\max}^{2} - \theta^{2}(\nu)},
  \label{eq:commonres}
\end{equation}
which is exact for the Gaussian beam used here. As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:residual}, this halves the raster's residual, while leaving the
drift's unchanged.

\section{Why a different cleaning basis cannot help}
\label{sec:rank}

In this section we consider whether a different choice of foreground
cleaning method could reduce the residual. Subspace methods such as PCA, ICA,
GMCA, NMF and kernel PCA all remove a rank-$n$ subspace of the
channel--channel covariance, and differ only in how that subspace is chosen.
% TODO cite: ICA/GMCA comparisons, e.g. Spinelli et al. (2022)
The relevant quantity is therefore the number of modes that each component
occupies, which we give in Table~\ref{tab:rank}.
